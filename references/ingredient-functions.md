# Ingredient functions as mechanisms

Do not treat ingredients as labels or one-for-one recipe slots. Infer how their composition changes gas-cell creation, batter flow, water availability, and heat-setting. A detailed causal model and sources are in [`cake-formation-science.md`](cake-formation-science.md).

| Ingredient/material | Mechanism(s) in cake formation | Important interactions |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat flour | Supplies starch that hydrates and gelatinizes during heating; supplies proteins that hydrate and can form gluten with mixing. | Water and mixing govern protein-network development. Too little structural material weakens gas-cell walls; excessive gluten development can toughen crumb. |
| Egg white | Water and heat-setting proteins; whipped proteins create an air/liquid foam that can supply bubbles. | Foam stability and gentle handling matter in sponge/foam methods; these functions are not equivalent to yolk's lipid interfaces. |
| Egg yolk | Water, proteins, and surface-active lipids; can contribute emulsification and gas-cell interface behavior. | A sponge-cake experiment found yolk lipids affected gas-cell incorporation/stability in its tested process; don't generalize to every cake. |
| Sugar | Dissolves in water; supplies sweetness, participates in browning, affects moisture and competes for available water. | Can delay starch gelatinization/protein setting in tested systems, changing the window for expansion and collapse. Reducing sugar alters more than taste. |
| Fat | Alters lubrication, tenderness, water distribution, mouthfeel, and gluten-network development. | Plastic solid fat may retain air during creaming; liquid oil does not enact the same aeration mechanism. State and method are coupled. |
| Water/milk | Hydrates flour and dissolves solutes; controls batter flow and contributes water available for vapor and starch gelatinization. | Milk also contains solids (and possibly fat); ingredient substitutions change both water and non-water solids. |
| Baking soda/powder | Chemical acid-base reactions can produce CO₂ bubbles. | Gas timing/quantity must match batter retention and setting. Soda requires adequate acid; powder includes acid/base components. Ratios are formulation-specific. |
| Acid, salt, flavor solids | Can change acid/base conditions, ionic environment, flavor, and total solids. | Citrus juice, cocoa, puree, syrup, etc. are not always functionally neutral flavor additions. |

## Causal reasoning sequence

1. Identify ingredient composition, quantity, and physical state; research unknown materials rather than guessing.
2. Map each ingredient to mechanisms, including overlapping roles.
3. Decide how bubbles will be introduced/generated and retained.
4. Check whether water and batter rheology support expansion, then whether starch/protein setting can stabilize the crumb.
5. Account for the selected cake architecture and method; a foam cake and creamed butter cake use different aeration routes.
6. State which ratios and outcomes are known, inferred, or unvalidated. The science explains plausibility; empirical baking is needed to establish a reliable formula.

Sources and limitations: [`../sources/source-notes.md`](../sources/source-notes.md) and the primary experimental studies cited in [`cake-formation-science.md`](cake-formation-science.md).
